I spent an hour auditing a pipeline before turning it back on, and the first thing I checked
was the number I was most confident about.

An append-only JSON file, one line per action taken. 1010 lines, zero parse errors. Counting
the rows that said an action was taken gave 322.

The real number was 293.

29 of those rows were not records of actions. They were notes that a previous run had left for
the next run, written into the same file because the file was there. To be accepted they
needed the file's fields, so they got `action: sent` and an id, and from that point on
nothing that counted could tell them apart from the real thing.

Every field was the right type. A schema validator passes this file today.

While I was in there I found the second one. Dedup works by matching a bare numeric id, and 6
real records were stored as `vendor-role-title-4443156560` instead of `4443156560`. The id is
right there on the end, and the lookup misses all six, so the pipeline would act on them a
second time. Acting twice looks exactly like acting once: no error, no retry, the second
action just succeeds.

Neither of these came from counting. Counting is what produced the wrong number. They came
from writing four lines that sort every row into exactly one bucket and then looking at the
buckets. Two buckets existed that should not have, with 29 and 6 rows in them.

The part worth keeping: a field being the right type is not the field being the right kind of
thing. Validators check types. They do not check whether a row is the kind of row the file is
for. Give one file two jobs and eventually the second job writes something wearing the first
job's shape.
